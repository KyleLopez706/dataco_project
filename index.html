What This Executive Overview Shows

This main view summarizes why 54.83% of DataCo orders arrive late and how machine learning predicts delays before dispatch. Each of the 6 visuals below answers one core business question and includes a plain-language takeaway note for Supply Chain Directors, Logistics Managers, and Warehouse Supervisors.

Visual 1: Delivery Status Breakdown

Question: What proportion of all dispatched packages arrive late versus on time?

Conclusion: Out of 180,519 orders, 54.83% (98,977) arrive late while only 45.17% (81,542) arrive on time.

Business Implication: Late delivery is the everyday norm across the company, exposing $20.13M in revenue to refund claims and customer churn.

Visual 2: Shipping Mode SLA Paradox (Promised vs. Actual Days)

Question: Which shipping tier has the worst delay rate, and how wide is the gap between promised and actual transit days?

Conclusion: First Class fails 95.32% of the time (1.00d promised vs. 2.00d actual) and Second Class fails 76.63% (2.00d promised vs. 3.99d actual), while Standard Class fails only 38.07% (4.00d vs. 4.00d).

Business Implication: Drivers are not slow—checkout promises are unrealistic. Shifting First Class promises from 1 day to 2 days resolves the paradox.

Visual 3: Monthly Order Volume & Delay Rate Timeline (2015–2018)

Question: Are shipping delays temporary seasonal spikes or a consistent year-round problem?

Conclusion: Across all 37 consecutive months, the late delivery rate stays locked between 53% and 57% regardless of monthly order volume.

Business Implication: Delays are not caused by holiday surges; permanent structural changes to carrier SLA rules are required year-round.

Visual 4: Geographic Market Order & Risk Breakdown

Question: Which regional markets handle the largest volumes and suffer the highest delay counts?

Conclusion: LATAM (51,594 orders) and Europe (50,252 orders) lead in volume, yet all 5 global markets have nearly identical late rates (54.36% to 55.21%).

Business Implication: Because domestic USCA orders fail at 54.80% just like international routes, leadership must fix internal scheduling rather than blaming customs.

Visual 5: Key Risk Drivers (ML Feature Importance)

Question: Which pre-shipment factors most heavily determine whether an order will be late?

Conclusion: Scheduled Shipping Days (39.9%) and Shipping Mode (35.2%) account for 75.1% of all predictive power, followed by Order Hour (5.2%).

Business Implication: Warehouse supervisors can accurately flag high-risk packages at checkout using only scheduling tier and intake time.

Visual 6: Predictive Model ROC Curve Comparison

Question: How effectively do our machine learning models separate late orders from on-time orders compared to baseline guessing?

Conclusion: HistGradientBoosting (AUC = 0.8135, Precision = 85.77%) and Random Forest (AUC = 0.8102) clearly beat Logistic Regression (0.7266) and Zero-R Baseline (0.5000).

Business Implication: High precision (85.77%) ensures dispatchers only spend expedited handling resources on genuinely at-risk packages.

What This Shipping & SLA Audit Shows

This section examines the Shipping Mode SLA Paradox in detail. It compares the delay rate across all four carrier tiers against their promised vs. actual delivery days, providing the exact data needed by Supply Chain Directors to renegotiate carrier SLAs.

Late Delivery Rate by Shipping Mode

Question: Which shipping tier has the highest percentage of delayed shipments?

Conclusion: First Class fails 95.32% of the time (26,513 of 27,814 orders), Second Class fails 76.63%, Same Day fails 45.74%, and Standard Class fails 38.07%.

Business Implication: Customers paying the highest rates for expedited shipping receive the worst delivery reliability.

Promised SLA Days vs. Actual Carrier Transit Days

Question: Why do First Class and Second Class fail while Standard Class succeeds?

Conclusion: First Class promises 1.00 day but takes 2.00 days (+1.00d overrun), and Second Class promises 2.00 days but takes 3.99 days (+1.99d overrun). Standard Class promises 4.00 days and takes 4.00 days.

Business Implication: Updating checkout promises by +1 to +2 days aligns customer expectations with physical carrier speed.

What This Regional & Segment Analysis Shows

This view tests whether shipping delays are caused by specific international markets, customer tiers (Consumer, Corporate, Home Office), or payment methods. It shows that delay rates are uniform (~54.8%) across every region and segment.

Order Volume & Late Delivery across 5 Global Markets

Question: Do international markets experience higher delay rates than domestic USCA routes?

Conclusion: Late rates remain within a 0.85% band across Europe (55.21%), Pacific Asia (55.05%), USCA (54.80%), Africa (54.59%), and LATAM (54.36%).

Business Implication: Delays are an internal scheduling policy issue rather than an international border bottleneck.

Customer Segment Fulfillment Performance

Question: Are Corporate B2B accounts protected from delivery delays compared to Consumers?

Conclusion: Consumer (51.80% share, 54.81% late), Corporate (30.35% share, 54.72% late), and Home Office (17.85% share, 55.07% late) suffer identical delay rates.

Business Implication: High-value Corporate B2B orders should receive priority dispatch screening to prevent account churn.

What This Financial & Product Analysis Shows

This view evaluates whether product profitability, order discounts, or specific merchandise categories influence late delivery risk. It confirms that delays are independent of product price or category (|r| < 0.01).

Order Profit Brackets vs. Late Delivery Rate

Question: Do high-margin orders receive faster, more reliable shipping than loss-making orders?

Conclusion: High-profit orders (>$100) experience the exact same ~54.8% delay rate as commercial loss orders (<$0).

Business Implication: Warehouse queues currently ignore order margin; high-profit orders should be prioritized during peak waves.

Top 10 Product Categories by Order Volume

Question: Which merchandise lines generate the highest fulfillment volume?

Conclusion: Cleats (24,551 orders), Men's Footwear (22,246), and Women's Apparel (21,035) lead volume, all with uniform 54%–55% delay rates.

Business Implication: Delays are not caused by bulky item dimensions or specific warehouse SKU zones.

2. Problem Statement & Zero-Leakage Data Cleaning Summary

Across 180,519 customer orders ($36.78M in gross sales), DataCo Global experiences a 54.83% late delivery rate ($20.13M in at-risk sales), driven by unachievable 1-day First Class (95.32% late) and 2-day Second Class (76.63% late) delivery commitments. Raw data (180,519 rows × 53 columns) was cleaned into 32 zero-leakage columns (dataco_cleaned.csv) with zero row loss:

Target Leakage Removed (3)
  • • Days for shipping (real)
  • • Delivery Status
  • • shipping date (DateOrders)
Duplicate Columns Removed (5)
  • • Benefit per order
  • • Sales per customer
  • • Order Item Cardprod Id
  • • Category Id & Order Customer Id
Empty / Sparse Removed (5)
  • • Product Description (100% null)
  • • Order Zipcode (86.2% null)
  • • Product Status (constant 0)
  • • Customer Email & Password
PII Identifiers Removed (6)
  • • Customer Fname & Lname
  • • Customer Street
  • • Product Image URL
  • • Order Item Id & Order Id
